# Why Your Business Might Be Invisible to AI Search

*Draft for In Business Magazine — October Technology page*
*Author: Yasir Memon, CEO & Founder, AZWebCorp (Gilbert, AZ)*
*~600 words. Third person throughout, except bullet 3, per RaeAnne's brief.*

---

A growing share of the questions that used to begin on a search results page
now begin in a chat window. A customer looking for a Chandler accountant or a
Tempe HVAC company may ask ChatGPT, Perplexity or Google's AI Overviews and
act on the answer without ever seeing a list of blue links. For Phoenix-area
businesses, that shift raises an uncomfortable question: when an AI assistant
describes your industry to a potential customer, does it mention you at all?

Ranking well on Google does not guarantee it. The two systems overlap, but
they are not the same, and a business can be perfectly healthy in one while
absent from the other.

The main reason is that AI systems reach websites through their own crawlers.
Googlebot has been visiting sites for two decades. The newer arrivals —
OpenAI's GPTBot and OAI-SearchBot, PerplexityBot, Anthropic's ClaudeBot, and
others — are separate programs that follow separate permissions. A site can
welcome Googlebot and quietly turn the rest away, usually without anyone
intending to. Security plugins, hosting-level bot filters and CDN rules
routinely block unfamiliar crawlers by default, and the block leaves no
visible trace on the website itself.

The second reason is that AI answers reward clarity over marketing. These
systems summarize. A page that states plainly what a business does, where it
operates and how to reach it is easy to summarize accurately. A page built
around slogans is not, and vague source material tends to produce vague
answers — or none.

Three checks a business owner can make this month, none of which require a
developer:

**1. Confirm AI crawlers are actually allowed.** Every site has a file at
yourdomain.com/robots.txt, viewable in any browser. It lists which automated
visitors may access the site. If names such as GPTBot, OAI-SearchBot,
PerplexityBot or ClaudeBot appear beside "Disallow," AI systems are being
turned away. Whoever maintains the site can change that in minutes. It is
worth deciding deliberately rather than by default.

**2. Make the basic facts unambiguous.** Business name, address, phone number,
hours and services should appear as plain text on the website — not only
inside images, PDFs or a contact form. Structured data, a standard format that
labels these details for machines, helps further. Inconsistencies matter too:
if the phone number differs between the website, Google Business Profile and
online directories, automated systems have no way to judge which is correct.

**3. In my own experience, the problem is often simpler than it looks.** When
I audited my own company's website earlier this year, I found that our
best-performing page in Google search — appearing in results for dozens of
queries — was blank. A headline with no content beneath it. It had been that
way for a long time, quietly earning visibility and no visits. I also found
several thoroughly written pages that Google had never once looked at, because
nothing else on our site linked to them. Being listed in a sitemap, it turns
out, is not the same as being found. We have since encountered the same
pattern on other Phoenix-area sites we have taken over, and it is now the
first thing we check.

None of this is exotic, and none of it requires new spending. It requires
someone to look. The businesses most exposed are not those with bad websites,
but those with sites nobody has examined closely in two or three years —
because the failure is silent. Nothing breaks. Traffic simply never arrives,
and the assumption is that more advertising is the answer.

Before increasing a marketing budget, it is worth confirming that the systems
now answering customers' questions can actually see the business at all.
