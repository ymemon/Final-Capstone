The physiology

What is actually happening under the hands.

Massage is sold with a lot of folklore. Here is the version that holds up: what pressure does to tissue, why it changes how much something hurts, and what it honestly cannot do.

It is a layered structure, and depth is a choice

Under your hand there is skin, then a loose fatty layer, then a tough sheet of connective tissue, then muscle inside its own sheaths. They are not one solid block. They are built to slide against each other, and a good deal of what people call tightness is layers that have stopped gliding freely.

Which layer a technique reaches is set by three things: how much force, at what angle, and for how long. A brisk surface stroke and a slow sustained lean are not the same treatment at different volumes — they are working on different tissue. That is most of the reason a session gets planned rather than improvised.

Pressure and pain do not share a wire

This is the part most people find genuinely surprising. Touch and pressure travel to the spinal cord along thick, insulated, fast nerve fibres. Dull aching pain travels along thin, slow ones. The difference in speed is roughly fiftyfold.

Both arrive at the same relay in the spinal cord, and the pressure signal gets there first. Heavy traffic on the fast fibres reduces how much of the slow pain signal is passed further up. It is the same reason your hand goes straight to a knock before you have thought about it: pressure genuinely does interfere with pain, at the spinal cord, before the brain is involved at all.

Sustained pressure also moves you out of a guarding state — breathing slows, heart rate settles, and the muscle stops bracing. A muscle that has stopped guarding moves further, and that change in available range is usually what people notice on the drive home.

Straight answers

What it does not do.

Three explanations you will hear in almost every massage room in the country, plus one more. None of them are true, and you deserve to hear that from the person taking your money.

It flushes out lactic acid

Lactate is a fuel the body reuses, not a waste product, and it clears on its own within about an hour of exercise. The soreness that turns up two days after a hard session is not lactic acid — it is microscopic muscle damage and the inflammation that follows. There is nothing there to flush.

It releases toxins

Nobody making this claim has ever named the toxin, measured it, or shown it leaving tissue during a massage. Your liver and kidneys do that job and need no help from an elbow. Drink water afterwards because it is a sensible thing to do, not because something is being rinsed out of you.

It breaks up scar tissue and adhesions

Dense connective tissue is remarkably strong for its cross-section, and the force needed to permanently deform it is well beyond what hands can produce. What does change is how the nervous system is regulating tone in the area — genuinely useful, and worth describing accurately.

It permanently lengthens muscle

Muscle length changes through sustained loading over weeks, not through an hour on a table. What improves within a session is your tolerance to being lengthened, which is why range of motion measures better afterwards. Real, useful, and a different claim.

What survives all that is still worth the hour: less pain, more usable range, a nervous system out of guarding, and an honest read on what is going on. This is general information rather than a diagnosis, and it does not replace seeing a doctor about a specific problem.

Your first visit

No mystery, no awkwardness.

Most people who have never had clinical bodywork hesitate because nobody tells them what actually happens. So here is the whole thing.

We talk first

A few minutes on what hurts, when it started, what makes it worse, and what you need to get back to doing. You stay dressed for this part.

Then we work

Hands-on treatment for the rest of the session, at a pressure you set. You are draped throughout, and you can change your mind about anything at any point.

You leave with a plan

What was found, what to do between now and next time, and an honest answer on whether you need to come back at all.

Questions

Before you book.

What does a session cost?

Sixty minutes is $95 and ninety minutes is $130. That is the whole price — there is no add-on menu, and nothing is sold to you partway through the session.

Which length should I book?

Book sixty if you can point at the problem. Book ninety if it is more than one area, if it is full-body, or if the pain moves around. If you book the wrong one it gets sorted out in the room, not held against you.

Is this a spa massage?

No. It is treatment-focused bodywork aimed at a specific problem. It can absolutely be relaxing, but the session is built around what is restricting you rather than around an hour of ambience.

Does deep tissue have to hurt?

No, and pressure that has you tensing up works against the session — a muscle bracing against you is a muscle that is not releasing. You set the pressure and it is adjusted whenever you say so.

What if I have an injury or a medical condition?

Mention it when you book and again at the start of the session. Some conditions change how the work is done, and a few mean it should wait or be cleared with your doctor first. Massage is bodywork, not a medical diagnosis — if something is getting worse, follows an injury you have not had looked at, or comes with numbness, weakness or pain that wakes you at night, see a doctor first.

How do I book?

Online at any hour, or by phone on (480) 944-0494 during opening hours. Online is usually quicker because you can see live availability.
